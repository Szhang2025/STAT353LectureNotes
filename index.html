01 Sample Space, Events & Counting ▼
Every probability problem begins with a clear description of what can happen. The sample spaceSample Space (S)The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. For rolling a die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. is the complete set of all possible outcomes of an experiment, and an eventEventA subset of the sample space. An event A occurs if the outcome of the experiment belongs to A. Example: "roll an even number" = {2, 4, 6}. is any subset of that sample space. When listing outcomes is impractical, we use counting rules — the multiplicative principle, permutations, and combinations — to count efficiently without enumerating everything.

📌 Key Definitions & Notation

  • Sample space (S): The set of all possible outcomes. Example: rolling a die → S = {1,2,3,4,5,6}.
  • Event: A subset of S. Example: A = "roll an even number" = {2,4,6}.
  • Union (A ∪ B): Outcomes in A or B (or both).
  • Intersection (A ∩ B): Outcomes in both A and B.
  • Complement (Aᶜ): Outcomes in S but not in A.
  • Disjoint: A ∩ B = ∅, so P(A ∩ B) = 0.

🎯 The Multiplicative Counting Rule

If a task is completed in k sequential steps, and step 1 has n₁ options, step 2 has n₂ options, …, and step k has nₖ options, then the total number of ways to complete the entire task is:

Total outcomes = n₁ × n₂ × … × nₖ

Why it works: For every choice in step 1, you can pair it with every choice in step 2, and so on. The options multiply rather than add because the steps are performed together, not as alternatives.

📝 Worked Example: Multiplicative Rule

A café offers 3 types of bread, 5 fillings, and 4 sauces. How many distinct sandwiches can a customer order?

StepChoiceOptions
1Bread3
2Filling5
3Sauce4
Total via multiplication3 × 5 × 4 = 60

🎯 Permutations vs. Combinations

Both rules count ways to choose r items from n, but they differ in whether order matters.

  • Permutation — order matters (e.g., first place vs. second place):
    P(n, r) = n!(n − r)!
  • Combination — order does not matter (e.g., choosing a committee):
    C(n, r) = n!r! (n − r)!
    The extra r! in the denominator removes the overcounting of the same r items in different orders.

📝 Worked Example: Arranging 5 White and 4 Black Balls

Suppose we have 5 identical white balls and 4 identical black balls. How many distinct ways can we arrange them in a row of 9 positions?

Step 1 — Identify the total number of positions. Total = 5 + 4 = 9.

Step 2 — Choose positions for the white balls. Since the white balls are identical, this is a combination:

C(9, 5) = 9!5! · 4! = 362,880120 × 24 = 362,8802,880 = 126

Check by choosing black positions: C(9, 4) = 126 — identical because C(9, 5) = C(9, 4).

Contrast with permutations: If the balls were distinguishable, we'd have 9! = 362,880 arrangements. Dividing by 5! and 4! gives 126.

Answer: 126 distinct arrangements.

📝 Additional Combination Examples

  • Lottery: Choosing 6 numbers from 49: C(49, 6) = 13,983,816.
  • Card hands: Choosing 5 cards from 52: C(52, 5) = 2,598,960.
  • Committee: Choosing 3 people from 10: C(10, 3) = 120.
  • Product mix: Choosing 2 of 6 paint colors: C(6, 2) = 15.
📌 Exercise 1 A bag contains 5 red, 4 blue, and 3 green marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement. (a) How many ordered outcomes are in the sample space? (b) How many outcomes are in the event "both marbles are the same color"? (c) How many outcomes are in the event "at least one red"?
✅ Detailed solution
  • (a) Sample space: Ordered draws without replacement: 12 × 11 = 132 ordered outcomes.
  • (b) Same color event: RR = 20, BB = 12, GG = 6. Total = 38.
  • (c) "At least one red" event: Complement Rᶜ = no red = 7 × 6 = 42. So |R| = 132 − 42 = 90.
  • Answer: (a) 132. (b) 38. (c) 90.
📌 Exercise 2 (Multiplicative Rule) A restaurant offers 4 appetizers, 6 main courses, and 3 desserts. How many different three-course meals can a customer order? How many if the customer may skip the appetizer?
✅ Detailed solution
  • By the multiplicative rule: 4 × 6 × 3 = 72 meals.
  • If skipping is allowed, the first step has 5 options: 5 × 6 × 3 = 90 meals.
  • Answer: 72 meals; 90 meals if skipping is allowed.
📌 Exercise 3 (Combinations — Marble Arrangements) In how many distinct ways can 7 identical white balls and 3 identical black balls be arranged in a row of 10 positions?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Total positions = 7 + 3 = 10. Choose which 7 positions hold the white balls.
  • C(10, 7) = 10! / (7! · 3!) = (10 × 9 × 8) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 720 / 6 = 120.
  • Check: C(10, 3) = 120. Same result.
  • Answer: 120 distinct arrangements.
📌 Exercise 4 (Combinations — Committee) A committee of 4 people must be selected from 11 volunteers. (a) How many distinct committees are possible? (b) If the committee must have a designated chairperson, how many ways are possible?
✅ Detailed solution
  • (a) Order doesn't matter: C(11, 4) = 11! / (4! · 7!) = 330.
  • (b) Choose chairperson first (11 ways), then 3 from the other 10: 11 × C(10, 3) = 11 × 120 = 1,320.
  • Answer: (a) 330 committees. (b) 1,320 ways with a chairperson.
02 Probability of an Event & Conditional Probability ▼
Once we have a sample space, we assign a number between 0 and 1 to each event to express how likely it is. When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes. Probability is always between 0 and 1, and the probability of the entire sample space is 1.

📌 Basic Rules of Probability

  • Range: 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
  • Sample space: P(S) = 1
  • Complement: P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)
  • Addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  • Disjoint events: if A ∩ B = ∅, then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

📌 Conditional Probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B),   provided P(B) > 0
  • Interpretation: We restrict attention to the reduced sample space B.
  • Multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B) · P(B)
  • Independence: P(A|B) = P(A) iff P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B).

📌 Exercise 1 (Simple Probability) A fair six-sided die is rolled twice. What is the probability that the sum of the two rolls is 7 or 11?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Sample space: 36 equally likely ordered pairs.
  • Event sum = 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) → 6 outcomes.
  • Event sum = 11: (5,6), (6,5) → 2 outcomes.
  • Total favorable: 6 + 2 = 8.
  • P(sum = 7 or 11) = 836 = 29 ≈ 0.2222
  • Answer: 2/9.
📌 Exercise 2 (Complement Rule) A card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. What is the probability it is a heart or a face card?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Hearts: 13 cards. Face cards: 12 total. Hearts that are face cards: 3.
  • By the addition rule: 13/52 + 12/52 − 3/52 = 22/52 = 11/26 ≈ 0.4231.
  • Answer: 11/26 ≈ 0.423.
📌 Exercise 3 (Conditional Probability) In a class of 40 students, 25 study math, 20 study physics, and 15 study both. A student is chosen at random. (a) What is the probability the student studies physics given they study math? (b) What is the probability the student studies math given they study physics?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Let M = math, P = physics. P(M) = 0.625, P(P) = 0.5, P(M ∩ P) = 0.375.
  • (a) P(P|M) = 0.375 / 0.625 = 0.6.
  • (b) P(M|P) = 0.375 / 0.5 = 0.75.
  • Answer: (a) 0.6. (b) 0.75. Conditioning is not symmetric!
📌 Exercise 4 (Independence Check) A card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. Let A = "the card is a King" and B = "the card is a face card." (a) Find P(A|B). (b) Are A and B independent?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Face cards = 12. Kings = 4. All Kings are face cards.
  • (a) P(A|B) = (4/52) / (12/52) = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333.
  • (b) P(A) = 1/13 ≈ 0.0769. Since P(A|B) ≠ P(A), the events are dependent.
  • Answer: (a) 1/3. (b) Dependent.
📌 Exercise 5 (Multiplication Rule) A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that both are red?
✅ Detailed solution
  • P(R₁) = 5/8. P(R₂|R₁) = 4/7.
  • P(R₁ ∩ R₂) = (5/8)(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14 ≈ 0.3571.
  • Answer: 5/14 ≈ 0.357.

📌 Exercise 1 Let A and B be events with P(A)=0.4, P(B)=0.5, and P(A∪B)=0.7. (a) Are they disjoint? (b) Are they independent?
✅ Detailed solution
  • (a) 0.7 = 0.4 + 0.5 − P(A ∩ B) → P(A ∩ B) = 0.2 ≠ 0. Not disjoint.
  • (b) P(A)P(B) = 0.2 = P(A ∩ B). Independent.
📌 Exercise 2 A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement. Are "first is red" and "second is blue" independent?
✅ Detailed solution
  • P(R₁) = 5/8. P(B₂|R₁) = 3/7 ≈ 0.4286.
  • P(B₂) = (5/8)(3/7) + (3/8)(2/7) = 21/56 = 3/8 = 0.375.
  • Since 0.4286 ≠ 0.375, dependent.

📌 Exercise 1 Machine A produces 60% of items, Machine B produces 40%. 3% of A's items are defective, 5% of B's are. An item is found defective. What is the probability it came from Machine A?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Prior: P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.4.
  • Likelihood: P(D|A) = 0.03, P(D|B) = 0.05.
  • Evidence: P(D) = 0.6(0.03) + 0.4(0.05) = 0.038.
  • Posterior: P(A|D) = 0.018 / 0.038 = 9/19 ≈ 0.4737.
  • Answer: Prior 0.6 → posterior ≈ 0.474.
📌 Exercise 2 (Medical Test) A disease affects 1% of a population. A test is 99% sensitive and 95% specific. If a person tests positive, what is the posterior probability they actually have the disease?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Prior: P(D) = 0.01.
  • Likelihood: P(+|D) = 0.99, P(+|Dᶜ) = 0.05.
  • Evidence: P(+) = 0.01(0.99) + 0.99(0.05) = 0.0594.
  • Posterior: P(D|+) = 0.0099 / 0.0594 ≈ 0.1667.
  • Answer: Prior 1% → posterior ≈ 16.7%.
📌 Exercise 3 Three urns: Urn 1 (50%) has 3 red/7 blue; Urn 2 (30%) has 6 red/4 blue; Urn 3 (20%) has 8 red/2 blue. A red ball is drawn. What is the probability it came from Urn 3?
✅ Detailed solution
  • Priors: P(U₁)=0.5, P(U₂)=0.3, P(U₃)=0.2.
  • Likelihoods: P(R|U₁)=0.3, P(R|U₂)=0.6, P(R|U₃)=0.8.
  • Evidence: P(R) = 0.15 + 0.18 + 0.16 = 0.49.
  • Posterior: P(U₃|R) = 0.16 / 0.49 ≈ 0.3265.
  • Answer: Prior 0.2 → posterior ≈ 0.327.
📌 Exercise 4 (Sequential Updating) A factory has two machines: A (70%, 2% defective) and B (30%, 6% defective). Two defective items found in a row. Update posterior after each.
✅ Detailed solution
  • Prior: P(A) = 0.7, P(B) = 0.3.
  • After 1st defect: P(D) = 0.032; P(A|D) = 0.4375.
  • After 2nd defect: New prior = 0.4375; P(A|D₁D₂) ≈ 0.2059.

05 Discrete Random Variables, Moments & Special Distributions ▼
A random variableRandom VariableA function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome in the sample space. A discrete random variable takes countable values. is a numerical summary of an experiment's outcome. A discrete random variable takes on a countable set of values, each with a specified probability.

📊 PMF, CDF & Moments

  • PMF: p(x) = P(X = x). Must satisfy p(x) ≥ 0 and Σ p(x) = 1.
  • CDF: F(x) = P(X ≤ x) = Σ_{k≤x} p(k).
  • Expected valueExpected Value (Mean) E(X)The long-run average value: E(X) = Σ x·P(X = x). (first moment):
    E(X) = Σ x · p(x)
  • Second momentSecond Moment E(X²)The expected value of X squared: E(X²) = Σ x² · p(x).:
    E(X²) = Σ x² · p(x)
  • VarianceVariance Var(X)Measures spread: Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]².:
    Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]²

🎯 How to Calculate the Second Moment E(X²)

  1. List every possible value of the random variable along with its probability.
  2. Square each value x to obtain x².
  3. Multiply each squared value by its probability: x² · p(x).
  4. Sum all the products to obtain E(X²).
E(X²) = Σ x² · p(x)

06 Continuous Random Variables ▼
A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval, so we describe its behavior with a density function. Probabilities are computed as areas under this curve, and the total area is always 1.

📈 Exponential, Normal & Z‑score

  • Exponential distributionExponential(λ)Waiting time between Poisson events. Mean = 1/λ.: f(x) = λe−λx, CDF F(x) = 1 − e−λx.
  • Normal distributionNormal(μ, σ²)The bell curve. ~68% within μ±σ, ~95% within μ±2σ, ~99.7% within μ±3σ.: Empirical rule 68‑95‑99.7.
  • Z‑scoreZ-scorez = (x − μ)/σ. Standardizes to N(0,1).: z = (x − μ) / σ.

🔗 Connection: Exponential ↔ Poisson

  • Poisson process: Events occur randomly at a constant rate λ.
  • Poisson counts the number of events in a fixed interval.
  • Exponential models the waiting time between consecutive events.
  • Key relationship: P(T > t) = e−λt = probability of zero events in time t.

⛓️ Application: Blockchain & Hashing

  • Poisson in blockchain: Blocks mined at roughly constant rate follow Poisson(λt).
  • Exponential in blockchain: The time until next block follows Exp(λ).
  • Hashing: Each hash is a Bernoulli trial with success probability p; the number of attempts follows a Geometric distribution with mean 1/p.

13 Foundations of Hypothesis Testing: Null, Alternative & Errors ▼
A hypothesis testHypothesis TestA formal procedure for deciding between two competing claims about a population parameter. answers the question: "Is the observed effect real, or could it have happened by chance?"

📝 How to Specify H₀ and Hₐ

  • Null (H₀): Status quo, contains equality. Example: H₀: μ = 100.
  • Alternative (Hₐ): Research claim. Contains ≠, <, or >.

Left‑tailedμ ≥ μ₀μ < μ₀Specifically smaller

⚠️ The Two Types of Errors

Power = 1 − β

  • Type I error (α): Rejecting H₀ when it is true. Analogy: convicting an innocent person.
  • Type II error (β): Failing to reject H₀ when Hₐ is true. Analogy: letting a guilty person go free.
  • Power (1 − β): Probability of correctly rejecting a false H₀. Aim for ≥ 0.80.
  • Trade-off: For fixed n, decreasing α increases β. The only way to reduce both is to increase n.

🎚️ How to Choose the Significance Level (α)

  • Conventional: α = 0.05 default; 0.01 strict; 0.10 exploratory.
  • Consider consequences: Smaller α if Type I error costly; larger α if Type II error costly.
  • Balance with power: Smaller α requires larger n.
  • Report the p-value: Give the exact p-value.
  • Pre-register: Choose α before collecting data.

✅ Detailed solution

  • (b) Drug lowers BP: H₀: μ ≥ 0; Hₐ: μ < 0 (left-tailed). Type I: approving a useless drug. Type II: rejecting a working drug.

Almost every hypothesis test for a mean or proportion uses the same core formula. Standardizing the difference between the observed statistic and the null value by the standard error tells us how many standard errors away the data are from the null hypothesis.

🎯 The General Test Statistic

test statistic = sample statistic − null valuestandard error

17 Connection: Hypothesis Tests ↔ Confidence Intervals ▼
Hypothesis tests and confidence intervals are two sides of the same coin. A two-sided test at level α rejects H₀ exactly when the hypothesized value falls outside the corresponding (1−α)×100% confidence interval.

🔗 The Duality

  • Decision rule: Reject H₀: μ = μ₀ at level α iff μ₀ is not in the (1−α) CI for μ.
  • Why they match: The CI contains all null values μ₀ for which |(x̄ − μ₀)/SE| ≤ critical value.
  • The CI gives more information: it shows the range of plausible values.

23 Applications: Gacha Probability & LLMs (Logit, N, T) ▼
The probability concepts developed throughout this guide appear in surprising real-world settings. Gacha games use the geometric distribution, while large language models rely on probability distributions over vocabulary tokens at every generation step.

🎮 Gacha: Wuthering Waves

  • Base rate: ~0.8% for 5★. X ~ Geometric(0.008).
  • P(X ≤ 10) ≈ 0.0771. E(X) = 125 pulls without pity.

🔑 Key Terms Explained: Logit, N, and T

1. What is the Logit?

The logit is the natural logarithm of the odds. If a probability is p, the odds are p / (1 − p), and the logit is:

logit(p) = ln p1 − p

In a language model, the raw output for each candidate token is called a logit score (often denoted z_i). It is not yet a probability — it can be any real number. The softmax function converts these logit scores into a valid probability distribution.

Why it matters: The logit scale allows the model to express very strong preferences (large positive logits) or strong dislikes (large negative logits) before normalizing to probabilities.

2. What is N?

N is the number of tokens in a sequence — the total count of words, subwords, or characters over which we are averaging. It appears in the cross-entropy and perplexity formulas because we compute an average loss across all tokens in the sequence.

L = − 1N Σ log P(tokeni)

For example, if a sentence contains 20 tokens, then N = 20, and we sum the log-probabilities of each of the 20 correct tokens before dividing by 20.

Why it matters: Without dividing by N, longer sequences would accumulate larger losses simply because they have more terms. Dividing by N makes the metric comparable across sequences of different lengths.

3. What is T (Temperature)?

T stands for temperature, a hyperparameter that controls how random or deterministic the model's output is. Before applying softmax, we divide each logit by T:

P(tokeni) = ezi / T / Σ ezj / T
  • T = 1: Standard softmax — the model's raw distribution.
  • T < 1: Sharper distribution — the model becomes more confident. As T → 0, it becomes greedy.
  • T > 1: Flatter distribution — the model becomes more random. As T → ∞, it approaches uniform.

Why it's called "temperature": The name comes from statistical physics, where temperature controls the energy distribution of particles. The math is identical.

🧠 Probability in LLMs (Summary)

  • Logits (z): Raw scores, any real number.
  • Softmax: P = ezi / Σ ezj.
  • Temperature (T): Divides logits before softmax.
  • N: Number of tokens in a sequence.
  • Cross-entropy: L = −(1/N) Σ log P(tokeni).
  • Perplexity: exp(−(1/N) Σ log P). Lower is better.
